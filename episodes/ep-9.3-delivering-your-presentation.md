## Think Like a Researcher

Aisha's slides are done, and her storyboard has been through three drafts. Then an email arrives from the seminar organiser: 
*"Good news: we'll run this as a hybrid session. Some students will join online. You'll have ten minutes, followed by five minutes of questions."*

Aisha reads it twice. Her slides are ready. But *is she*?

She pictures a student at the back of the room asking a question that nobody online can hear. She pictures a camera, and she isn't sure where to look. 
And, if she's honest, she pictures someone asking something about her study that she simply doesn't know the answer to.

Having good slides and being ready to present are two different things. Let's try to close that gap.

## A Wise Researcher Once Said…

_In his guide to preparing scientific presentations, Daniel Günther names "authenticity and individuality" as two of the most important ingredients of a successful presentation._

That's good news if you're nervous about "performing". You are not being asked to become a different person on stage, or to be the most prepared. Remember that presenting your findings is an act of generosity.  

## Part 1: Getting Ready

### Practise out loud

Most people prepare by polishing slides and then reading them over in their heads. That isn't practice. Real practice means you:

- **Say it out loud, standing up, with a timer.** Your mouth needs rehearsal just as your slides do. If you run over, *cut* content rather than speaking faster. Staying within your time is a mark of respect for the audience and for the speakers after you.
- **Rehearse for a small test audience.** Friends, family, lab mates, or classmates all work. They will show you what's unclear, and what questions are likely to come up.
- **Record yourself.** It's uncomfortable, but revealing: you'll spot filler words, rushed sections, and places where your energy dips.
- **Practise your transitions.** The moments *between* slides are where many talks wobble. 
- **Practise in the real setting if you can.** For virtual talks, that means rehearsing on the actual platform.

For your notes, use short cue words rather than a full script. Reading a script word for word tends to sound rehearsed and takes away the personality that makes your talk unique.

### Nerves

Nearly everyone feels nervous before presenting, including experienced researchers. You can't switch nerves off, but you can work with them. Prepare thoroughly (knowing your material is the best remedy for the fear of forgetting), slow your breathing before you start, and arrive early so the space feels familiar. Look for one or two friendly faces so it feels like a conversation, and remember that your audience came to learn something and wants you to do well.

### Start and end on purpose

Plan your first and last 30 seconds. Open with something that makes people curious, such as a short story, a surprising fact, or a question. Close not with a bare summary but with the **implication**: what your findings mean and what you'd like your audience to do (the call to action).

## Part 2: Delivering Live

Whether you're in a lecture hall or under a mango tree, the craft of speaking has a lot in common. The following tips are helpful tips to keep in mind:

- **Vary your pace.** Slow down for the ideas that matter and speed through familiar details. A predictable rhythm lulls listeners while a varied one holds them. Use **pauses** deliberately, as a breath for the audience as well as for you.
- **Give new ideas extra time.** To you, your study is familiar, but to the audience, it's brand new. When you reach a fresh or tricky idea, slow down and say it clearly.
- **Let your enthusiasm show.** Interest is contagious. Let your love for the sibject matter shine through in your presentation.
- **Ask rhetorical questions, tell stories, and add a small surprise.** A question followed by a pause makes people think. Short stories, metaphors, and everyday comparisons make ideas stick, and an unexpected fact keeps people on their toes.
- **Lead your slides; don't let them lead you.** Say your point *before* you click to the next slide. If you advance first, the audience might read ahead and stop listening.
- **Use your whole voice and body.** Avoid a monotone, make eye contact with different parts of the room, and use gestures that support your words rather than distract from them.

### A note on informal settings

At Aisha's community meeting, the rules of delivery stretch rather than disappear. She would speak in the language people use daily, greet the room in line with local custom, keep her tone conversational, and invite reactions as she goes. Informal audiences often respond out loud, which is good: it tells you immediately what's landing and what isn't.

## Part 3: Delivering Across Modes

### Live online (virtual)

Presenting through a screen removes much of the feedback you'd get from faces and body language, so you may have to work harder to stay connected.

- **Look at the camera**, not at your own picture, and place it near eye level.
- **Protect your audio.** Poor sound loses an audience much like a plain slide. Use a headset if you can, mute notifications, and find a quiet spot.
- **Tell people how to take part** (chat, raised hand, or at the end?), check in at planned points, and use short polls to see what people know. Then **wait longer than feels comfortable** for answers.
- **Agree on cameras.** In a small group, cameras on boosts interaction; in a large one, cameras off reduces lag, especially in low connectivity settings.
- **Plan for weak connections.** Share slides ahead of time, and send large videos in advance rather than streaming them.
- **Recruit a co-host** or "helper" for larger sessions, to watch the chat and run polls.

### Hybrid

In a hybrid session, your audience is in two places at once, and the risk is that one group gets overlooked (left out?). A few habits help both feel included:

- **Welcome both audiences** at the start and say how each can take part.
- **Alternate your gaze** between the room and the camera.
- **Repeat every question** before answering. Online viewers can't hear the back of the room, and the room can't hear a typed question.
- **Use a "voice of the chat":** a colleague who reads online questions aloud to the room.
- **Give online participants extra time**, and sometimes let them speak first.
- **Check your slides on a small screen**, since text that works on a projector can vanish in a streaming window.

### Recorded and on-demand

Recording gives you an advantage live talks rarely do - the opportunity to try again. But this comes at the cost of losing the audience's presence.

- **Speak to one person.** Imagine a single colleague watching and speak to the lens as if to them.
- **Bring extra energy.** Recorded voices can sound flat, so let a little more melody into yours.
- **Keep it short and state your headline early.** Viewers can leave at any moment.
- **Check the sound before the picture.** Record a short test and play it back on your phone.
- **Provide a way to respond.** Since you can't take live questions, add an email address, comment thread, or form, and then actually reply.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: callout

### 💡 Three habits that work in every mode

1. **Say how people can take part** (questions, chat, hands, or after the talk).
2. **Repeat each question** so everyone knows what is being answered.
3. **Test your tools** before the audience arrives, and keep a backup plan.

## Part 4: Making the Audience Part of It

Not every presentation is a talk. In some, the audience does as much of the work as you do.

### Workshops and teaching sessions

A workshop is an informal, two-way format in which participants *do* things. A well-loved structure is to give a short piece of input, then an activity, then a short discussion, repeating the cycle as needed. One simple tool is **think-pair-share**: ask a question, let everyone think quietly for a minute, have them discuss with a neighbour (or in an online breakout room), and then invite a few to share with the whole group. It works with any group size, needs no technology, and gives quieter people time to think before they speak.

Another good move is to ask the audience to **make a prediction** before you reveal a result. Aisha could ask: "Which homes do you think had more malaria?" and let people discuss before showing her chart. People remember answers better when they've committed to a guess first.

### Seminars and webinars

These are usually more formal and speaker-led, with questions at the end. Thank the organisers and chair, state any conflicts of interest after your title slide, and arrange for useful links to be pasted into the chat for online viewers. A long talk paired with a few minutes of Q&A at the end may feel passive. So, consider punctuating it with a poll or a pair discussion.

### Poster sessions

A poster session is a live, two-way conversation, and what you say beside your poster matters as much as the poster. Prepare a **poster pitch** of about a minute (the headline, the story, and one invitation such as "Does this match what you've seen?") and a **longer version** for visitors who stay. Greet people and ask what brought them over, to better understand their interest. Treat their comments as a gift especially because a poster session is often a good chance to get feedback before publication.

To practise, try a role-play in the spirit of activities used to prepare student researchers for poster sessions. One partner presents while the other plays a different visitor each round: curious but from another field, in a hurry, sceptical, or silently reading. Swap and compare notes.

### Lightning talks and pitches

A three- or five-minute lightning talk should not a long talk run at double speed. Choose **one** idea, open with your hook, and spend your time on the headline and its evidence. Also prepare a *very short* pitch of your work (what you'd say in a corridor) alongside a longer one, so you're ready for whatever chance comes up.

## Part 5: Answering Questions and Receiving Feedback

Questions are not an exam at the end of your talk, but are an indication that someone was listening, and they're a chance to learn from people who see your work with fresh eyes.

### Listening is half of presenting

Active listening means giving the questioner your full attention, letting them finish, and trying to hear what they really want to know. Resist the urge to plan your whole answer while they're still speaking.

### Consider these recommendations for each question

1. **Listen** all the way through without interrupting.
2. **Pause.** Take a breath. This gives you time to think and lets the rest of the audience absorb the question.
3. **Repeat or rephrase** the question for everyone, in the room and online. It confirms you heard and understood, buys thinking time, and helps you articulate responses better.
4. **Answer briefly and honestly:** Start by looking at the person who asked, then widen your gaze to include everyone. Long answers tend to lose people.
5. **Move on, or offer to continue afterwards** if the question needs a longer conversation.

### Opening the floor

Instead of "Any questions?", try "What questions do you have?" or "What can I clarify for you?" This phrasing assumes questions exist and is easier to answer. If the room is quiet, give people a minute to think or even talk with a neighbour first, as this can bring out questions that nobody would ask alone. Before the talk, prepare for likely questions, and keep your **backup slides** from Episode 9.2 ready.

### When you don't know

At some point, someone will ask something you can't answer. That's normal, and handling it can earn you trust. Reflect on the example below:

> "Thank you, that's an important question. I haven't looked at that in my data, so I don't want to guess. Can I find out and follow up with you after the session?"

Say this when you actually plan to follow up. A sensitive audience can tell when you're merely bluffing or trying to change the subject. Say this when you actually plan to follow up.
