## Think Like a Researcher

Aisha's slides are done, and her storyboard has been through three drafts. Then an email arrives from the seminar organiser: 
*"Good news: we'll run this as a hybrid session. Some students will join online. You'll have ten minutes, followed by five minutes of questions."*

Aisha reads it twice. Her slides are ready. But *is she*?

She pictures a student at the back of the room asking a question that nobody online can hear. She pictures a camera, and she isn't sure where to look. 
And, if she's honest, she pictures someone asking something about her study that she simply doesn't know the answer to.

Having good slides and being ready to present are two different things. Let's try to close that gap.

## A Wise Researcher Once Said…

_In his guide to preparing scientific presentations, Daniel Günther names "authenticity and individuality" as two of the most important ingredients of a successful presentation._

That's good news if you're nervous about "performing". You are not being asked to become a different person on stage, or to be the most prepared. Remember that presenting your findings is an act of generosity.  

## Part 1: Getting Ready

### Practise out loud

Most people prepare by polishing slides and then reading them over in their heads. That isn't practice. Real practice means you:

- **Say it out loud, standing up, with a timer.** Your mouth needs rehearsal just as your slides do. If you run over, *cut* content rather than speaking faster. Staying within your time is a mark of respect for the audience and for the speakers after you.
- **Rehearse for a small test audience.** Friends, family, lab mates, or classmates all work. They will show you what's unclear, and what questions are likely to come up.
- **Record yourself.** It's uncomfortable, but revealing: you'll spot filler words, rushed sections, and places where your energy dips.
- **Practise your transitions.** The moments *between* slides are where many talks wobble. 
- **Practise in the real setting if you can.** For virtual talks, that means rehearsing on the actual platform.

For your notes, use short cue words rather than a full script. Reading a script word for word tends to sound rehearsed and takes away the personality that makes your talk unique.

### Nerves

Nearly everyone feels nervous before presenting, including experienced researchers. You can't switch nerves off, but you can work with them. Prepare thoroughly (knowing your material is the best remedy for the fear of forgetting), slow your breathing before you start, and arrive early so the space feels familiar. Look for one or two friendly faces so it feels like a conversation, and remember that your audience came to learn something and wants you to do well.

### Start and end on purpose

Plan your first and last 30 seconds. Open with something that makes people curious, such as a short story, a surprising fact, or a question. Close not with a bare summary but with the **implication**: what your findings mean and what you'd like your audience to do (the call to action).
